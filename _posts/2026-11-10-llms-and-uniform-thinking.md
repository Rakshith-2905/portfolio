Try this before you read on. Open any chatbot and type: *"Pick a random number between 1 and 10. Reply with only the number."*

It said 7, didn't it?

It is not a coincidence, and it is not your chatbot. In a test that asked each model a hundred times, GPT answered 7 on 92 occasions and Claude on 90. Gemini answered 7 every single time. A controlled study of six models, seven languages and six temperature settings (75,600 queries) found that 7 is "the preferred value by far for every single model". GPT-4o-mini, Gemini 2.0 and Phi-4 chose it about 80% of the time. A truly random pick would land on 7 one time in ten.

Most people laugh this off as a party trick: computers are bad at randomness. That explanation is wrong, and the right one is the most useful thing I know about where AI-assisted writing is heading.

## 1. The 7 problem

Here is the twist. **People pick 7 too.** In a classic 1976 experiment, Kubovy and Psotka asked 558 people for a random digit, and 28% chose 7. That is almost three times what chance predicts. Psychologists call it the "blue-seven" effect. Seven feels random. It is odd, it is prime, it is not at the edge and not in the middle.

So the chatbot did not invent a bias. It learned ours from the text we wrote, and then amplified it. We can say exactly how much. Suppose, as a simple model, that people put probability \\(h_7 = 0.28\\) on 7 and spread the rest evenly, about 0.08 on each other digit. (Kubovy and Psotka asked for a digit from 0 to 9; I assume the same preference carries over to 1 to 10.) A model that raises human preferences to a power \\(k\\) and renormalizes, which is what low-temperature decoding and preference tuning both do (Section 3), says 7 with probability

<div class="math">\[ p_7(k) \;=\; \frac{h_7^{\,k}}{h_7^{\,k} + 9\,h_{\text{other}}^{\,k}} . \]</div>

Solving for \\(k\\) gives \\(k = \ln\!\big(9\,p_7/(1-p_7)\big) \,/\, \ln(h_7/h_{\text{other}})\\). The 80% rate in the controlled study corresponds to \\(k \approx 2.9\\). The 90% and 92% rates correspond to \\(k \approx 3.5\\) and \\(3.7\\). **Chatbots pick numbers the way people do, cubed.**

Cubing sounds harmless until you measure what it does to choice. Count the *effective number of options*, \\(e^{H}\\), where \\(H\\) is Shannon entropy. It is how many equally likely options would leave you just as uncertain. People choosing a digit behave as if choosing among 8.8 options. A model at 80% behaves as if choosing among 2.6, and one at 92% among 1.6. **Ten numbers have collapsed to fewer than two.**

The same thing happens wherever you look:

- **Jokes.** Jentzsch and Kersting asked ChatGPT for a joke 1,008 times. Over 90% of the replies were the same 25 jokes. The scarecrow that won an award for being outstanding in his field came back 140 times.
- **Coin flips.** Van Koevering and Kleinberg asked GPT and Llama models to flip coins. More than 88% of sequences began with heads, a stronger version of the human habit of starting with heads. They concluded that the models "not only replicated human biases, but exacerbated them."

That sentence is the whole essay. Now replace "a number between 1 and 10" with "a cover letter", "a performance review" or "an argument about social media".

## 2. From numbers to paragraphs



A random number is a toy. The question is whether the same pull shows up when the output is a story, an essay or an opinion. Here is the evidence, restricted to controlled studies and large corpus analyses, with the numbers as the authors report them.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/posts/oped/oped-evidence.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Bar charts: measured convergence in four controlled studies, and the estimated share of LLM-modified abstracts in four corpora">
  <figcaption>Left: convergence in controlled writing studies. The metrics differ (embedding similarity, unique key points, unique n-grams), so compare the direction of the bars, not their lengths. Right: estimated share of LLM-modified abstracts. Sources are listed at the end.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Individuals improve while the collection narrows.** Doshi and Hauser ran an experiment with 300 writers and 600 judges. Writers who could see up to five AI-generated ideas produced stories rated 8.1% more novel and 9% more useful than writers working alone, and the least creative writers gained the most (10.7% on novelty). Yet the stories of writers who saw a single AI idea were 10.7% more similar to one another than the unassisted stories. Every individual moved up, and the cloud of stories shrank.

**The narrowing is in ideas, not just words.** Padmakumar and He had 38 professional writers produce 300 argumentative essays alone, with base GPT-3, or with instruction-tuned InstructGPT. The model wrote about a third of the characters in assisted essays. With InstructGPT, the fraction of unique *key points* fell from 0.941 to 0.877, a 6.8% loss, and the fraction of unique 4-grams fell from 0.973 to 0.953. The base model showed no significant effect. That contrast matters, and Section 3 explains why.

**The narrowing has a direction.** Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha gave 118 participants in India and the US a GPT-4o autocomplete. Cosine similarity between Indian and American essays rose from 0.48 (SD 0.11) to 0.54 (SD 0.13), with *t*(6958) = −17.89, *p* < 0.001 and Cohen's *d* = −0.44. In the authors' words, AI suggestions led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles. Both groups also wrote about 30–35% faster. Speed and sameness arrived together.

**Opinions move too.** In Jakesch et al. (N = 1,506), people who wrote with an assistant quietly biased toward one side of a question were 2.04 times more likely than controls to argue that side (95% CI [1.83, 2.30]), and their own stated attitudes shifted afterwards.

**The exposure is already large.** Liang et al. analysed 950,965 papers and estimated that by February 2024 up to 17.5% of sentences in computer-science abstracts were substantially LLM-modified. Kobak et al. looked at 15.1 million PubMed abstracts and put a *lower bound* of 13.5% on 2024 abstracts processed with an LLM. They found it by counting words that suddenly became too frequent. "Delves" appeared 28 times more often than its pre-LLM trend predicts.

Two honest caveats. First, these effect sizes are small to medium. *d* = 0.44 is not a cultural extinction event. Second, most of these are short-horizon lab studies. The interesting question is not the size of the effect today. It is what happens when a small per-use effect compounds across billions of uses and several model generations. That requires a model of the mechanism.

## 3. Why the machine pulls toward the middle

### 3.1 Sharpening: the math of "most likely"

A language model defines a distribution \\(p(y)\\) over continuations \\(y\\). What we read is not \\(p\\) itself but a *decoded* version of it. With temperature \\(T\\), the model samples from

<div class="math">\[ p_T(y) \;=\; \frac{p(y)^{1/T}}{\sum_{y'} p(y')^{1/T}} . \]</div>

For \\(T<1\\), raising probabilities to a power greater than one concentrates mass on the head of the distribution. To see how fast, take a Zipf-distributed space of 10,000 ways to phrase an idea (\\(p(y)\propto r^{-1.1}\\) by rank \\(r\\), the shape word frequencies typically follow). Measure diversity as the *effective number* of phrasings, \\(e^{H(p)}\\), where \\(H\\) is Shannon entropy. It counts how many equally likely options would give the same uncertainty.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/posts/oped/oped-temperature.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Effective number of phrasings and head versus tail probability mass as a function of temperature">
  <figcaption>Zipf(1.1) over 10,000 phrasings. Moving from T = 1.0 to T = 0.7 cuts the effective number of phrasings from 328 to 16, a 95% reduction. The 10 most common phrasings go from 41% to 81% of all output, and the tail beyond the top 100 shrinks from 35% to 5%.</figcaption>
</figure>

The diversity loss is not linear in temperature. It is roughly exponential, because the exponent \\(1/T\\) acts on every probability at once. This is the same operation as the \\(k\\) in the 7 example, with \\(k = 1/T\\). A cubed preference is the same thing as decoding at \\(T \\approx 0.3\\).

### 3.2 Alignment is sharpening by another name

Most deployed assistants are fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The standard KL-regularized objective has a known closed-form optimum:

<div class="math">\[ \pi^*(y) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,\exp\!\big(r(y)/\beta\big), \]</div>

where \\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\\) is the pre-trained model, \\(r\\) is the learned reward and \\(\beta\\) sets how far the policy may drift. Now add one empirical fact. Human raters prefer text that feels familiar, so the reward contains a typicality term, \\(r(y) = r_{\text{true}}(y) + \gamma\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\\) with \\(\gamma>0\\). Zhang et al. (2025) formalize this "typicality bias" and find it in preference data. Substituting gives

<div class="math">\[ \pi^*(y) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)^{\,1+\gamma/\beta}\;\exp\!\big(r_{\text{true}}(y)/\beta\big). \]</div>

That is the temperature equation, and the 7 equation, again, with effective temperature \\(T_{\text{eff}} = 1/(1+\gamma/\beta) < 1\\). **Alignment that rewards familiarity sharpens the model permanently, even when you sample at T = 1.** This is the cleanest available explanation for the Padmakumar result: the base model did not homogenize essays, and the instruction-tuned one did. It also matches Kirk et al.'s finding that RLHF substantially decreases output diversity compared with supervised fine-tuning alone.

### 3.3 Population diversity: a variance decomposition

Sharpening explains why one model is narrow. Society-level sameness also depends on how many people use it and how much they defer to it. Represent each writer's style as a point \\(x\\) in some embedding space. Unassisted writers are spread with mean \\(\mu\\) and variance \\(\sigma^2\\). A fraction \\(\alpha\\) of writers adopt a model whose default style is \\(m\\) and blend toward it with weight \\(w\\): \\(x' = (1-w)\,x + w\,m\\). The law of total variance gives the population variance exactly:

<div class="math">\[ \operatorname{Var}(x') \;=\; \underbrace{(1-\alpha)\,\sigma^2}_{\text{non-adopters}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha(1-w)^2\sigma^2}_{\text{adopters}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha(1-\alpha)\,w^2\,(m-\mu)^2}_{\text{between groups}} . \]</div>

A model trained by maximum likelihood on human text places its default near the human mean, so \\(m\approx\mu\\), and the last term vanishes:

<div class="math">\[ \frac{\operatorname{Var}(x')}{\sigma^2} \;=\; 1-\alpha\,w\,(2-w). \]</div>

Three consequences follow, each testable:

1. **Diversity falls with the product of adoption and deference.** At universal adoption (\\(\alpha = 1\\)) and the one-third model share that Padmakumar and He observed (\\(w \approx 0.33\\)), the formula predicts a 55% variance loss.
2. **People resist far more than the formula assumes.** The measured key-point loss in that same study was 6.8%, about eight times smaller. Writers are not passive blenders. They reject, edit and redirect. That gap is the most hopeful number in this essay, and it is the thing worth protecting.
3. **Model pluralism adds diversity back.** If adopters split across \\(K\\) models whose defaults \\(m_k\\) are spread with variance \\(\tau^2\\), an extra \\(\alpha w^2\tau^2\\) appears on the right-hand side. A monoculture of one or two foundation models sets \\(\tau^2\approx 0\\).

The pairwise-similarity metric used in the studies connects to this directly. For unit-normalized embeddings, \\(\mathbb{E}\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2 = 2(1-\bar s)\\), where \\(\bar s\\) is the mean pairwise cosine similarity, so \\(1-\bar s\\) is proportional to total variance. Agarwal et al.'s shift from 0.48 to 0.54 is therefore an 11.5% contraction of the spread between Indian and American writing, from a single writing session.

### 3.4 The loop closes: training on our own output

The final mechanism turns a one-time effect into a compounding one. As more text is model-written, future models train on it. Shumailov et al. showed in *Nature* that models trained recursively on generated data lose the tails of the original distribution first. The core of the result fits in a few lines. Fit a Gaussian by maximum likelihood to \\(n\\) samples drawn from the previous generation's fit. Then

<div class="math">\[ \sigma^2_{t+1} \;=\; \sigma^2_t\cdot\frac{\chi^2_{n-1}}{n}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\log\sigma^2_{t+1}-\log\sigma^2_t\big] \;=\; \psi\!\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right)+\log\tfrac{2}{n} \;\approx\; -\frac{2}{n}, \]</div>

where \\(\psi\\) is the digamma function. The log-variance is a random walk with negative drift, so the median variance decays like \\(e^{-2t/n}\\) and the variance reaches zero almost surely. The *mean*, \\(\big(\tfrac{n-1}{n}\big)^t\sigma_0^2\\), decays only half as fast, which is exactly how averages can hide a collapse that most individual runs have already suffered.

### 3.5 What fresh human writing buys

The pure recursion is the worst case. In reality, each new corpus mixes human text with model text. Let \\(q\\) be the human distribution of ideas, \\(\alpha\\) the synthetic share of the corpus, and \\(M\\) any training-plus-decoding operator. Then

<div class="math">\[ p_{t+1} \;=\; (1-\alpha)\,q \;+\; \alpha\,M(p_t). \]</div>

Two bounds hold for *any* \\(M\\), however sharp. The mass on any set of rare ideas \\(A\\) satisfies \\(p_{t+1}(A)\ge(1-\alpha)\,q(A)\\). And because entropy is concave, \\(H(p_{t+1})\ge(1-\alpha)\,H(q)\\). Fresh human writing puts a hard floor under diversity, and the floor is proportional to the human share.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/posts/oped/oped-feedback.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Effective number of ideas and tail mass over 60 generations for synthetic shares from 0% to 100%">
  <figcaption>Simulation: Zipf(1.1) over 10,000 ideas; each generation trains on 50,000 samples, decodes at T = 0.9, and its output becomes a share α of the next corpus. Results at a 50% synthetic share: 130 effective ideas (down from 328), with the tail at 25% of the corpus (down from 35%). At 90%: 3.9 effective ideas, with the tail at 5.3%. At 100%: a single idea. The simulated values stay above the analytic floors (18 and 1.8 effective ideas at 50% and 90%).</figcaption>

The shape of this curve is the argument of the essay. Up to roughly half synthetic, diversity degrades gracefully. Past about three-quarters, it falls off a cliff. Gerstgrasser et al. reach the same qualitative conclusion for real models: collapse is avoided when real data *accumulates* alongside synthetic data rather than being replaced by it.

## 4. From emails to ideas

Put the pieces together and the trajectory is not a vibe; it is arithmetic. Decoding and alignment sharpen each model (\\(T_{\text{eff}}<1\\)). Adoption and deference multiply that narrowness across the population (\\(\alpha w\\)). A handful of vendors removes the pluralism term (\\(\tau^2\to 0\\)). Recursive training compounds the loss across generations, limited only by the human share of new text (\\(1-\alpha\\)). Emails converge first because they are the most delegated. Arguments follow, because we accept the model's framing along with its sentences (Jakesch). Ideas follow last, because the space of things that feel sayable shrinks toward the space of things the model finds probable.

## 5. This is not an argument against LLMs

I use these tools every day, and the company I help lead builds AI tutors. The Doshi and Hauser result cuts both ways. The least creative writers gained the most, and Agarwal et al.'s participants wrote about a third faster. Fluency and access are real goods. Modernist architecture had real virtues too: cheap, fast, hygienic housing at scale. The failure was letting the default become the only option. The goal is not to stop using the tools. It is to keep \\(w\\) small, \\(\tau^2\\) large and \\(1-\alpha\\) healthy.

## 6. What to do, mapped to the variables

**Individuals: keep \\(w\\) small.**

- *Think first, prompt second.* Write your position in your own words before opening a chatbot. The model should stress-test your idea, not originate it. That is the difference between \\(w\approx 0.1\\) and \\(w\approx 0.9\\).
- *Sample the distribution, not the mode.* Ask for five very different approaches with rough probabilities, including ones the model thinks are bad. Zhang et al. report that this "verbalized sampling" prompt recovers much of the diversity alignment removes.
- *Keep your fingerprints.* The 8× gap between predicted and measured homogenization is people editing. Keep editing.
- *Write some things with no assistance at all.* Every unassisted page is a contribution to \\(q\\), the floor in Section 3.5.

**Product builders: measure it like a regression.**

- *Ship a homogenization metric.* For a fixed prompt set, report mean pairwise cosine similarity \\(\bar s\\) across users, or the Vendi score, against an unassisted human baseline. Treat \\(\Delta\bar s>0.02\\) as a failing test, the same way we treat a latency regression.
- *Default to the user's draft.* Interfaces that open with a generated draft maximize \\(w\\). Interfaces that start from the user's words and offer targeted edits minimize it.
- *Expose the temperature.* Let people choose between "polished" and "divergent", and show them what it does.
- *Teach rather than replace.* In tutoring, the best interaction is often a question back to the student. [[ANECDOTE: a concrete design decision from your tutoring work that favors student reasoning over model output. Keep it free of confidential metrics.]]

**Institutions: protect \\(1-\alpha\\) and \\(\tau^2\\).**

- *Schools* should grade process as well as product: outlines, drafts, oral defenses and in-class writing.
- *Employers* should stop paying for polish as a proxy for thought.
- *Publishers and platforms* should keep provenance and protect clearly human-authored spaces, for readers and for the training data of every future model.
- *AI labs* should publish diversity evaluations next to capability benchmarks, audit preference data for typicality bias (the \\(\gamma\\) in Section 3.2), and accumulate rather than replace human data.
- *Policymakers* should treat a concentrated model market as a diversity risk as well as a competition risk, because it is the \\(\tau^2\\) term.

## 7. What we do not know yet

Rigor includes stating the limits. The controlled studies are small (N = 38 to 1,506), short, and mostly English, and they measure diversity with different embeddings, so their effect sizes are not directly comparable. The variance model is linear and treats deference \\(w\\) as fixed, whereas real deference probably grows with habit. The simulations use a Zipf toy distribution, not real text. They show the *shape* of the dynamics, not a forecast. Nobody has yet measured \\(\alpha\\) for the web as a whole, or how fast it is rising. These are answerable questions, and they deserve the same funding as capability benchmarks.

## Pick 4

Seven is not a bad answer. It is the most human answer, made more human than any human. That is precisely the danger. A world of 7s is not wrong in any single case. It is just a world where nobody picks 4, nobody picks 10, and the joke about the scarecrow is the only joke. Cities went the same way: thousands of reasonable choices, made with the same tools, added up to identical glass towers. Ideas are next unless we choose otherwise. Progress has always lived in the tail: the improbable sentence, the minority view, the person who framed the problem differently. The math above says the tail is exactly what these systems remove first, and that only fresh human writing reliably puts it back.

LLMs are the most powerful writing tools we have built. Used carelessly, they give us a world of perfectly reasonable 7s. Used deliberately, they give everyone the fluency to build something of their own. The variables are known. Let's set them on purpose.
